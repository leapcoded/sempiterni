# Atlas Faelan

*Lives in: Characters → Protagonists (supporting). Companion to Wren Emberlain (partner, emotional anchor of Wren's early arc), Healthcare (CSD and the fatigue analogue, suppressant access), Economy and Enforcement (Cyph, tier, what a crash costs practically), and the Violet Branch (performance authorisation, Sprawl underground music scene). The unnamed Sprawl tavern where he occasionally plays is a location placeholder — naming deferred.*

---

## What He Is

Atlas Faelan is a fox, Wren's partner, and a musician who cannot work as one. He lives in the specific, unglamorous territory between those last two facts — someone whose identity is entirely bound up in something his body no longer lets him do reliably, who has not stopped being that person and has not found a way to be someone else instead, and who would resist being described in either of those terms because he is also, genuinely, more than the gap between what he was and what he can manage now.

He is not bitter. He is not resigned. He is someone who has learned, through years of negotiation with his own limits, to be very honest about what a day is and what it isn't — who knows, by mid-morning, roughly what kind of day it will be, and who has built his life around that knowledge without letting the knowledge become the life.

## The Conditions

Atlas carries two things, independently and in combination.

### The Fatigue

The closer analogue is chronic fatigue — not tiredness, not the kind of exhaustion that sleep fixes, but a systemic unreliability that presents as crash-and-recovery cycles. On ordinary days, he functions within a ceiling that is lower than most people's but real and workable. On crash days, he does not. The distinction between these is not always visible from the outside until it is very visible, and one of the things Atlas has learned to do, not always successfully, is to notice the approach of a crash before it arrives rather than after.

He pushes. This is not recklessness — it is a considered, recurring decision to live within reach of the things that matter to him rather than at a safe distance from everything that might cost him. He knows what pushing costs. He pays it and then recovers and then makes the same calculation again, slightly updated by the last time. Wren has never asked him to stop. Wren has also never pretended not to notice.

### The CSD

Atlas's Cordis Sensitivity Disorder is stable — has been stable for years, is not currently progressing, is managed with suppressant. He does not access this through the Ivory Branch's official provision; at Sprawl tier, that route is not reliably available to him. It comes through unofficial channels — The Margin's medication supply network or something adjacent — which means it arrives, but not on a schedule he controls entirely. Stage Two presentation: the Gloaming at the periphery of his vision, opalescent, more intrusive in low light or when he is depleted. On ordinary days it is background noise, something he's learned to look past. On crash days, it is not background noise.

This is the interaction that matters. The fatigue and the CSD are not the same condition and do not share a cause, but they are not independent of each other either. A bad fatigue crash pulls resources from every system, and the CSD is a system. Atlas has learned to read his fatigue partly through what the Gloaming does — a flare at the edge of his vision, intensifying, is often the first sign that he has pushed past what the day had to offer. The warning comes slightly too late to be fully useful, but it comes.

He is matter-of-fact about both conditions in a way that has taken years to earn. He does not perform stoicism and he does not perform suffering. He describes what's happening with the same flat accuracy he'd use to describe the weather, and then he deals with it, and then he moves on. This is not suppression. It is the specific, hard-won equanimity of someone who has made peace with something that is not going away.

## Music

Atlas was a musician before he was someone who could no longer sustain it as work. He still is, in every way that is not the practical one.

He plays a violin — or the in-world equivalent, pending a naming pass. The most exposed bowed instrument: nothing to lean into on a bad day, nowhere to hide. It will betray a tremor, a flagging bow arm, a mind that isn't fully present, before the player has admitted any of those things to themselves. He knows within the first minute of picking it up whether a performance is possible. This is, probably, not incidental. Atlas plays the things that demand everything, even now, even knowing what it costs.

He plays occasionally at a Sprawl tavern — unnamed, pending a naming pass — that operates at the edge of Violet Branch's enforcement reach, the kind of venue where the line between authorised and unauthorised performance is managed by mutual convenience rather than formal clearance. He is not a regular in any schedule a Branch officer could track. He plays when he can, when a day is good enough and the set feels worth the crash that will follow, and when he does it he is fully himself in a way the bad days don't allow.

Wren goes. Wren has always gone. This is not discussed between them in terms that would make it a thing to be acknowledged, which is exactly how Atlas needs it to be. Wren sits somewhere they can see him and watches, and whatever Wren is on those nights — careful, accommodating, the one who manages the gap between good days and bad ones — they are not that. They are just watching. Atlas does not need managing up there. He needs an audience, and Wren is one.

The crash after a set is usually two days. Wren knows this. Atlas has never asked Wren to track it. Wren tracks it anyway, in the particular, unannounced way they track everything that matters.

## The Relationship

Atlas and Wren came together gradually, in the way that things come together when both parties are people who do not rush toward vulnerability. The conditions were not disclosed early. They emerged as the relationship deepened, as Atlas became someone who no longer needed to perform capability around Wren, and as Wren became someone Atlas trusted with the days that weren't good ones.

Wren learned Atlas's tells before Atlas told them anything directly. This is consistent with who Wren is — the person who notices what doesn't fit a pattern — and Atlas, once he understood what Wren had learned and when, was not unsettled by it. He was, in the quiet way that is his register for most things, relieved. Being known without having to explain yourself is a specific kind of intimacy, and Atlas, who has spent years managing the careful work of not being defined by his conditions in rooms full of people who would define him by them the moment they knew, found in Wren someone who learned the fact and then proceeded to know him anyway.

The care runs both ways, which is important. Wren's attentiveness is real and Atlas receives it — but Atlas also knows Wren, knows the specific shape of Wren's moods and the way Wren's precision sometimes tips into something tighter and more anxious than precision, knows when to leave things alone and when to push gently and when to simply be present without making presence into a project. He is not a passive recipient of Wren's care. He is the person who knows Wren best, which means he is, without either of them naming it this way, also watching.

The irony that the document on Wren names but does not resolve: Wren has spent years learning to offer steadiness from what they believe is a position of safety — someone on the outside of bodily betrayal, exempt from the kind of unreliability Atlas lives with. That framing was never true. Wren's own body and mind were rewritten once already. Atlas does not know this. Wren does not know it either. What Atlas knows is the Wren who exists now, the Wren who was assembled after, and he loves that person completely — which means, when the buried history eventually surfaces, there will be a question about what Atlas is loving and whether the answer changes. It should not change. It will feel, to both of them, like it might.

## What Wren Notices First

The progression question the to-do names is less about the CSD — which is stable, not currently changing — and more about the fatigue, and about Wren's attentiveness to the gap between what Atlas presents and what is actually true.

What Wren notices first, before any single event marks a change, is a shift in the recovery pattern. The crashes are not more frequent. The crashes are not worse. But the recoveries are taking slightly longer than they used to — a day that was previously enough is now not quite enough, and Atlas returns to function fractionally later than the baseline Wren has been quietly tracking for years. Atlas has not said anything. Atlas may not have noticed, or may have noticed and made the calculation that it does not yet warrant naming.

Wren holds this the way they hold everything they notice and have not been given permission to name: carefully, without acting on it, waiting to understand whether it is signal or noise. It is the same skill that makes them an excellent archivist. It is also, in this context, a specific form of suffering — knowing something is shifting and not knowing what it means and not being able to ask without making it into a conversation Atlas hasn't chosen to have yet.

This is not a dramatic escalation. It is the quiet, accumulating weight of watching someone you love and not being sure whether what you're seeing is real.

## Death

Atlas is killed in Act Two B by the Fifth Division — Obsidian Branch's covert arm — acting on authorisation from above. He is taken against his will, tortured, and left for dead. Wren finds him broken and disfigured. He is past speaking. He dies in silence, without a last conversation, without a goodbye.

He could manage his conditions. He could not manage what was done to him. That is the point. Everything Atlas built — the sovereignty over his body, the careful negotiation with his limits, the philosophy of pushing and paying and pushing again — is dismantled in one act by an institution that did not know and did not care what it was destroying. The control he spent years constructing is taken from him by people for whom he was never a person, only a lever.

His death is a message directed at Wren. The Fifth Division leaves him to be found. This is deliberate — not an execution designed to disappear someone, but a warning designed to be read. Wren reads it. Wren also, in the silence of finding him, recovers the buried memory of what they did ten years ago. The grief and the guilt arrive simultaneously. There is no one left to help carry either.

Atlas knew the Wren who was assembled after the memory procedure. He loved that person completely and never knew what he was loving. He dies without knowing. Wren gets the truth and loses the witness in the same moment — the one person who might have helped them survive knowing is already gone.
