# Bonding and Family Traditions

*Lives in: World & Lore → Core Systems. Companion to Arbour Branch Employment (Branch as vocation-identity shapes bonding conventions), Arbour Economy and Enforcement (ARC registration and Cyph implications), Arbour Law and Punishment (household as legal unit), Wayfarer Divergence (the species-blind, authority-by-wisdom culture that underpins Wayfarer bonding norms), and the three Wayfarer branch documents (each branch's specific ceremony flavour). This document covers the shape of family life and formal bonding across Cordis's two main cultural traditions — Arbour and the Free Territories — as it actually functions, not as any official position would describe it.*

---

## Arbour

### What a Bond Is

In Arbour, a formal bond — the closest thing to a marriage — is simultaneously an ARC record and a social event. Both are present. Which one does more of the work depends entirely on where you are in the city.

The ARC registration is the institutional layer: a logged entry in civil records, administered through the Tabularium, that establishes two individuals as a legal household. From the ARC record flow a set of practical consequences: Cyph allocation is adjusted (the household rate, not two individual rates), tier status is formally reconciled where there's a mismatch, shared property and inheritance rights attach, and any children born into or adopted by the household acquire their tier classification through the record. This is what the Council cares about. From the state's perspective, a bond that isn't registered didn't happen.

The social ceremony is the human layer: whatever the community around the bonded pair does to mark it. This ranges from elaborate to minimal depending on tier, Branch affiliation, and individual preference, and it exists entirely in the community rather than in any institutional framework. ARC does not care about the ceremony. The ceremony does not require the ARC registration. But in practice, the two tend to happen in proximity, because most people want both the community recognition and the legal protections.

### How It Stratifies

**Luminary and upper Meridian** — the formal ceremony is more elaborate and the ARC registration is completed carefully and promptly. Branch affiliation shapes the ceremony's texture: Azure Branch households tend toward reserved, structured observances that echo the Branch's ritual-engineering culture; Verdant Branch favours communal meals and seasonal timing (a bond witnessed at the growing season's peak carries a particular resonance for a Branch organised around agricultural cycles); Violet Branch produces the most performative ceremonies, sometimes indistinguishable from the Branch's broader relationship with staged occasion. The ceremony here is social capital. Guest lists are political. Witnesses are chosen deliberately. The ARC record is handled by someone who knows how to handle it correctly.

**Meridian broadly** — ceremony is retained but scaled to practical means. Often a gathering at whatever space the pair can access: a Branch common room, a Meridian tavern with a backroom, a shared courtyard. The ARC registration is still completed, because at this tier the practical consequences of not completing it (tier complications, Cyph adjustment, inheritance clarity) matter enough to make the bureaucratic cost worthwhile. The ceremony is warmer and less formal than Luminary; it is also less about capital and more about the people actually present.

**Sprawl** — the community-witnessed bond is the real thing. ARC registration is often not completed: the cost in Cyph is real, the Tabularium is not physically accessible without transit costs, and the relationship between Sprawl residents and the administrative apparatus that controls their tier allocation is, at best, cool. What actually holds a Sprawl bond together is whether the people around the pair recognise it — the same mechanism that holds most things in the Sprawl together. The practical consequences of not registering are real but often accepted: no formal Cyph adjustment, no tier inheritance clarity for children, no legal household status. Sprawl families exist entirely and function entirely without ever appearing in the civil record in any formal sense.

This means Sprawl children's tier classification is messier than Luminary children's — often defaulting to whatever their individual ARC civil record carries, which may not reflect their actual family situation. This is not a bug in the system. The system has no particular incentive to fix it.

### Cross-Tier Bonds

A formal bond between partners of different tiers creates an administrative question: what does the household record say? ARC resolves this, in practice, by registering the household at the lower partner's tier unless an appeal process is completed — and the appeal process costs Cyph, takes time, and requires documentation most Sprawl residents don't have. The practical effect is that upward mobility through bonding is, officially, possible, but the administrative friction ensures it rarely produces a clean outcome. The lower-tier partner doesn't simply acquire the higher tier by marriage; the household tier is negotiated with the civil record, and the outcome depends on whether anyone at the Tabularium is motivated to be helpful.

The social picture is more complicated than the administrative one. Cross-tier bonds exist, and the city does not formally prohibit them. But a Luminary resident bonding with a Sprawl resident is a legible social statement in both directions — downward-facing in the Luminary community, upward-reaching in the Sprawl community, and neither of those readings is entirely comfortable for the pair navigating them. The practical realities compound it: where do you live? Whose community do you live inside? Who comes to the ceremony, and what do they make of each other? A Luminary/Sprawl bond is not impossible. It is simply difficult in the specific, grinding way that everything across the tier gap is difficult — not one big obstacle, but a hundred small ones that don't stop.

### Species

Arbour has no formal restrictions on who can bond with whom by species. The ARC record does not categorise by species for bonding purposes. This is the official position, and it is consistently held.

The social reality is that certain species pairings receive quiet but real disapproval in parts of Arbour's population — the flavour and intensity of which varies considerably by tier and community. The disapproval is not institutionally enforced and not universal; it does not produce legal consequences; it does produce the kind of ambient friction that shapes what feels possible versus what technically is. It is an inheritance from pre-crash Earth history that survived the crash in a form no one in Arbour explicitly endorses but many implicitly reproduce. The specifics of which pairings carry this weight are not recorded anywhere, because no official body acknowledges the phenomenon exists.

### Children and Household

Children — biological or adopted — acquire their tier classification from the registered household record, with all the complications this produces for unregistered Sprawl households (see above). The household is the practical unit for raising children in Arbour; extended family matters more in Luminary (where Branch legacy, sponsor networks, and accumulated material position are worth maintaining across generations) and significantly less in the Sprawl (where the nuclear or near-nuclear unit is simply what survival organises around).

Arbour does not have a formal, culturally recognised marker for adulthood. The functional transition to adult life is determined by Branch entry or employment (typically mid-to-late adolescence), which varies by Branch and by what the family can support. In practice, Sprawl children enter adult working life substantially earlier than their Luminary counterparts — not because any rule requires it, but because the household Cyph situation often does.

## The Free Territories

### Wayfarer Bonding

Among the Wayfarers, a bond is a caravan matter. The ceremony is witnessed by the caravan community, spoken in the oral tradition's register — which means the bonding vow, whatever its specific language for a given branch, is not a document. It is a statement made in front of people, to be carried in memory, the way everything that matters in Wayfarer culture is carried. There is no equivalent of the ARC record. There is no institution to register with. The bond exists because the caravan says it does, and continues to exist as long as the caravan's memory of it does.

The practical question any Wayfarer bond must answer is: which caravan? If both partners belong to the same caravan — which is common, given that caravans are the primary social world people spend their lives in — this is not a question. If the partners come from different caravans, one must join the other's. Which partner moves is a genuine negotiation, informed by which caravan needs them more, what skills and relationships each partner would be bringing across, and, frankly, what each partner is willing to give up. This negotiation happens between the individuals, their respective caravans, and often the elder leadership of both. It is not always resolved tidily.

**Inter-branch bonds** — as Mira and Doran's pairing (Aeolian and Corvane) demonstrates — are not unusual and carry no stigma. The Wayfarer tradition has no species hierarchy and no branch hierarchy; the branches are understood as different emphases within the same culture, not competing systems. A Corvane trader marrying into an Aeolian caravan brings their relationship-keeping expertise with them; an Aeolian scout marrying into a Thessaly caravan brings their ranging knowledge. The caravan gains something from the exchange either way. The question is only which caravan, not whether the pairing is acceptable.

**Species** is not a variable. Species-blind structure is the one value the Wayfarer tradition has never negotiated on, and it applies as completely to bonding as to everything else.

### Coming of Age

The transition from child to full caravan member is marked at a specific age — around fifteen to sixteen years by the general Wayfarer reckoning, though the precise number varies slightly between branches and between individual caravans. What doesn't vary is that the transition is marked: a ceremony, witnessed by the caravan, in which the person being marked is formally acknowledged as someone whose word counts, whose judgment is their own to exercise, and whose contribution to the caravan is no longer supplementary.

The form the ceremony takes differs by branch. Aeolian caravans tend toward a practical demonstration embedded in the ceremony — the new adult leading a portion of a ranging trip, reading weather or terrain, making a call in front of the elders and having it acknowledged as theirs to make. The ceremony is then the verbal recognition of what just happened, not a ritual detached from what the person can actually do. Thessaly caravans mark it more formally — a composed statement of values and commitment, delivered before the caravan in the tradition of the branch's controlled-outward culture, the performance of composure being itself the demonstration. Corvane ceremonies tend to involve an account of a relationship successfully maintained — a trade, a contact kept, a debt honoured — offered as evidence that the person is ready to carry the caravan's reputation in the world.

What all three share: the ceremony is the community acknowledging the person, not an institution assigning them a classification. There is no document. There is no record. There is only the caravan's memory that this happened, and the person's own.

Children who are taken in from outside the caravan — as Pell was into Long Reach — are not exempt from this marker, nor are they given it early as compensation for the circumstances of their entry. They wait, they train, they demonstrate, and they receive it when the caravan judges they are ready. That judgment is made by the elders, as all significant judgments in Wayfarer culture are, and it is final in the way that all elder judgment in Wayfarer culture is final — not appealable to any outside authority, because no outside authority exists.

### Fixed Settlements and Non-Wayfarer Communities

The communities of the Wayfarer continent that are not Wayfarer — the fixed settlements, the non-Wayfarer nomadic groups — have their own traditions around bonding and family that this document does not presume to enumerate. They are their own thing, developed across three centuries of their own circumstances.

What can be noted is that fixed settlement bonding conventions tend to more closely resemble Wayfarer conventions than Arbour conventions — there is no ARC, no administrative record, no Cyph to restructure. Community recognition is the mechanism available, and it is what most of these communities use. The specific forms vary as much as the settlements themselves.
