# Arbour — Economy and Enforcement

## Part One — Cyph

### What It Is

Cyph is Arbour's currency. It is not a physical object — no coin, no token, no card. It is a **logged claim**: a number attached to your ARC registry entry that determines what you can access, spend, and receive. You do not carry Cyph. The system carries it for you.

The name is worn-down from *scrip* through three centuries of Sprawl pronunciation and usage — the emergency resource-claim system the founding generation improvised from ARBOUR|05's existing allocation infrastructure, now so thoroughly the only economic reality anyone has known that nobody currently alive thinks of it as improvised at all.

### Origin — ARC's Inherited Function

Cyph was never designed. It emerged.

ARBOUR|05's Autonomous Routing & Control system was built to allocate resources across the ship's systems and population during the voyage — food, water, power, consumables, all tracked and distributed by ARC according to role, need, and availability. When the ship crashed and the founding generation had to organise survival under genuine scarcity, nobody sat down and invented a currency. They simply kept using the allocation system that was already running, because it was the only infrastructure capable of tracking who was owed what.

What ARC issues, Arbour calls Cyph. What ARC thinks you're owed — based on criteria inherited from the original ship-role hierarchy and compounding across three centuries of degraded memory — becomes what you can spend. The currency is, at its foundation, **ARC's resource allocation outputs interpreted as money**.

### The Registry

Every Cyph-eligible resident of Arbour exists in ARC's registry. The registry traces directly back to ARBOUR|05's original passenger manifest — the seed from which every subsequent entry grows. Births are added through a civil registration process administered through the Tabularium's lower public floors; deaths are flagged and the entry suspended. The registry is the bureaucratic definition of citizenship: to exist in ARC's record is to exist in Arbour's economy.

**Civil registration is a service, not a right.** The Tabularium administers it, which means access to registration is subject to the same queue, cost, and institutional friction as any other Tabularium service. A Sprawl family that cannot navigate the registration process — through illiteracy, cost, distance, or simple institutional hostility — produces an unregistered child. That child grows up invisible to ARC, which means invisible to Cyph, which means invisible to the formal economy entirely. Not poor. Absent.

### Allocation by Tier

ARC's allocation logic is built on ship-role ancestry — what your lineage contributed to ARBOUR|05's functioning determined your baseline draw, and that baseline has compounded and calcified across ten generations into something that broadly, consistently favours the descendants of high-status ship roles. ARC did not create the tier hierarchy. It inherited it and kept running it, which amounts to the same thing.

- **Luminary** — high, stable allocation. Sufficient for genuine comfort, accumulation, and the kind of economic insulation that makes falling out of the upper tier feel like a remote abstraction rather than a lived risk.
- **Meridian** — mid, variable allocation. Enough for ordinary life; not enough for real security. Meridian residents are the tier most acutely aware of the gap between what they have and what Luminary has, because they can see it clearly from where they stand.
- **Sprawl** — low, inconsistent allocation. Sufficient, in official accounting, for survival. Insufficient, in practice, for anything beyond it. Inconsistencies in ARC's outputs hit the Sprawl hardest, because there is no buffer — a corrupted allocation cycle that shortchanges a Luminary resident by a few percent is an inconvenience; the same error applied to a Sprawl household is a crisis.

### ARC Degradation — The Accumulating Problem

ARC's memory core has degraded non-linearly over three centuries. Allocation errors are real, increasing, and impossible to audit completely because the data showing the acceleration is itself stored in corrupted memory. Individual errors are quietly managed by the Branches — adjusted, reclassified, smoothed over before they surface publicly — but no Branch currently has visibility of the aggregate drift. The system as a whole is getting worse in ways nobody is tracking, because tracking it would require a functional version of the same system that's failing.

The practical result: Cyph allocation has always been slightly arbitrary at the margins, and is becoming more so. In the Sprawl, where the margins are the whole story, this is experienced as ordinary instability — a feature of the economy rather than a malfunction. Nobody in the Sprawl expects the allocation to make consistent sense. They have built their lives around that inconsistency.

## Part Two — The Off-Grid Economy

### Unregistered Residents

An unknown but significant portion of Sprawl residents are not in ARC's registry. Some were never registered at birth. Some arrived in Arbour from outside its territory — from the Free Territories, from Wayfarer caravans, from communities beyond the formal boundary — and were never entered into a system that had no procedure for them. Some were registered and subsequently had their entries suspended for reasons that may or may not have been their doing.

These residents have no Cyph. They are not, in any meaningful sense, poor — poverty implies a position on a scale the system acknowledges. They are simply outside the scale. The formal economy does not interact with them. They interact with each other.

### Private Wallets — Running Cold

The Sprawl's self-taught engineering culture — descendants of engineers who arrived at understanding from below, through necessity and salvage rather than controlled transmission — eventually produced people who understood ARC's allocation infrastructure well enough to build parallel to it. Private wallet systems exist: off-registry Cyph storage and transfer infrastructure that moves value without logging it in ARC's record. The practice is called **running cold** in Sprawl vernacular — cold as in untracked, off the heat of the official system.

Running cold is not straightforward to set up. Access to the tools and knowledge required runs through the same informal networks as everything else the Sprawl keeps off the official record. Otis Audagar controls significant portions of this access — not just the wallets themselves, but the knowledge of how they work and who can be trusted to run them.

A cold transfer is untaxed, unmonitored, and unrecoverable if something goes wrong. It is also the only way to move value between people the formal system cannot see.

### Barter and Knowledge Exchange

Alongside cold transfers, the off-grid economy runs on barter and on the exchange of technical knowledge — schematics, failure reports that were officially reclassified, the names of people who know things. In the Sprawl, knowing how a specific section of conduit actually behaves is worth more than any official accounting of that knowledge, because the official accounting doesn't exist. This is the economy Otis Audagar sits at the centre of: not just goods, but *understanding*.

## Part Three — Being Posted

### What It Is

When the Obsidian Branch determines that a resident has committed an infraction warranting public sanction, they initiate a **posting**. Obsidian files the action; the **Department of Communications** publishes it. The two functions are deliberately separate — different paper trails, different institutional hands — though in practice they coordinate closely and the gap between initiation and publication is rarely more than a day.

A posting is a public record containing:
- The resident's name and registry identifier
- The stated infractions
- The duration of the posting
- The associated Cyph action (reduction or freeze, scaled to severity)

It is not a conviction in any judicial sense. It is a publication. The posting *is* the punishment, in the same way that a name on a list *is* a social fact, regardless of what the person named did or didn't do.

### Where It Appears

**Community hubs** are the primary posting point — physical boards at local gathering points, water access points, market areas. The posting reaches a Sprawl resident through their immediate community before it reaches them through any other channel. Neighbours know first.

**Data pads** carry the posting digitally, but the data pad network is not evenly distributed. Coverage scales with tier: sparse and unreliable in the Sprawl (often broken, often outdated, sometimes running information weeks old), present and functional in Meridian's main thoroughfares, dense and comprehensive in Luminary. A Luminary posting is near-total and automatic; a Sprawl posting is social and navigable, if you have the right relationships.

**Job comms** — the employment communication infrastructure — flag posted residents automatically. Every job application, contract query, and work contact carries the posting status. This is frequently the most economically devastating element: the Cyph action reduces what you can spend, but the job comms flag reduces what you can earn.

### How It Lands by Tier

The same posting hits differently depending on where you live.

In the **Sprawl**, a posting is social before it is administrative. Your community hub board goes up before the data pad updates. Some neighbours quietly help. Some avoid you. Some use the information as leverage. The job comms flag matters less if your work was already off the books. The Cyph reduction matters more, because there was less to begin with.

In **Meridian**, the posting is semi-automated and semi-social — the data pad reaches people alongside the community board, the job comms flag is immediate and reliable, but there are still human edges to navigate.

In **Luminary**, the posting is algorithmic and near-inescapable. The data pad coverage means you cannot move through your neighbourhood without the record following you. The job comms integration is seamless. The community is less likely to quietly help, because the social risk of association is more legible and more immediately costly. Being posted in Luminary is quieter and more total than being posted anywhere else.

### The Department of Communications

The body responsible for publishing postings, maintaining the data pad network, and administering job comms infrastructure. Its position within the Branch structure is to be determined through the Branch deep-dives (see *Open Follow-Ups*); it functions as a distinct department with its own operational mandate, separate from Obsidian Branch, though the two coordinate closely on posting publication.

The Department of Communications also controls what the data pad network displays beyond postings — public announcements, approved information, the Council's official communications. The patchiness of the network in the Sprawl is not an accident: a data pad that doesn't reach a Sprawl district is a data pad that can't carry information *into* that district either, which suits the Council's information architecture exactly as well as it suits their budget.

## Part Four — Tier Movement

### The Official Position

Tier movement is officially possible in both directions. The Council maintains this position — and it is not dishonest, exactly. People do move. Some people have moved. The official position simply declines to dwell on the numbers.

### Moving Up

Upward movement requires one of a small number of recognised pathways:

**Branch promotion** — the most common legitimate route. A Sprawl resident who joins a Branch at junior level and advances far enough can earn a formal tier reclassification. This is neither easy nor fast. Branch employment itself requires registration, sponsorship, and enough Cyph to sustain yourself during the probationary period. The promotion that triggers a tier reclassification is years further along still. Most Branch employees spend their entire careers without crossing a tier boundary; the ones who do are exceptional enough to be genuinely remembered.

**Marriage** — a formal union with a registered resident of a higher tier triggers an eligibility review. The review may or may not result in full reclassification; partial reclassification (adjusted allocation, revised residential eligibility) is more common than a clean tier jump. The process is slow, means-tested, and sometimes overturned after the marriage ends.

**Fame** — the Violet Branch administers cultural life in Arbour. Sufficiently prominent cultural achievement — a performer, an artist, a figure who becomes genuinely known across tiers — can trigger a formal recognition. The Violet Branch nominates; the Council confirms. This path is real, and extraordinarily rare, and the people who achieve it tend to be held up constantly as proof that the system works, which should tell you something about how often it happens.

**Medals and commendations** — formally awarded for bravery or exceptional public service. The branch awarding the commendation can request a tier reclassification alongside it. These are real mechanisms. They are administered by institutions with significant interest in how few of them they issue.

### The Cost of Moving Up

Upward tier movement is not free. When ARC's registry is amended to reflect a new tier, two costs apply.

The **flat amendment fee** is a one-time charge: the administrative cost of altering an ARC record, payable in Cyph before the reclassification takes effect. The amount is significant enough that it is, for most Sprawl residents seeking it, one of the largest single Cyph outlays they will make. The official rationale is that maintaining registry integrity requires administrative resource. The practical effect is that someone who has just barely achieved the conditions for reclassification must also have Cyph in reserve to pay for it — a requirement that eliminates a meaningful fraction of otherwise eligible people at the final step.

The **ongoing higher-tier living costs** are permanent. A higher tier allocation sounds like more Cyph, and it is — but it comes attached to higher costs for housing, for resource access, for the social infrastructure of the tier you've entered. Meridian costs more to live in than the Sprawl. Luminary costs more than Meridian. A Sprawl resident who has moved up is now paying Meridian prices on Meridian allocation, with no Sprawl-era savings buffer to absorb the transition. Some people return to the Sprawl voluntarily within a few years. Some tip into managed decline while technically still registered at their new tier.

### Moving Down

Downward movement is not administered as a sudden drop. It is a managed decline — a compounding of costs that the system neither accelerates nor interrupts.

A Luminary resident who has been posted, lost their income, or found their allocation reduced still retains their tier registration while ARC's record goes unchallenged. They continue to pay Luminary prices. Their allocation falls short of those prices. Debt accumulates. ARC's record begins to reflect the shortfall. When the gap becomes formally untenable — when the accumulated debts and reductions reach a threshold ARC is compelled to act on — a tier reclassification is triggered automatically.

By then, the resident has already been living the reality of the tier below for some time. The formal reclassification is the last step, not the beginning of the fall. **The fall happens first. The paperwork catches up.**

The accumulation of debt, small reductions, compounding shortfalls, and social costs — the job comms flag, the posting, the social distance of neighbours who read ARC records and know what they're looking at — has a name in the Sprawl: *death by a thousand cuts*. Nobody on their way down has a single bad day. They have a thousand ordinary days that get slightly worse.

### Debt and Lending

ARC is the sole formal lender in Arbour. There is no bank, no licensed moneylending body, no institutional creditor distinct from the system that already administers Cyph. To borrow formally is to borrow from ARC — which means the same entity that determines your allocation, records your tier, and administers your posting status also determines how much you can borrow and on what terms.

A loan request is submitted through the Tabularium. ARC weighs the request against the resident's current parameters: registered employment, tier and habitation costs, dependents, existing allocation, posting history, tier movement record. The output is a lending limit — the maximum ARC calculates you can repay across future allocation cycles — and a repayment schedule, automatically deducted from subsequent cycles. The interest is not called interest. It is called an **allocation processing fee**, embedded in the deduction schedule and never itemised separately.

Because ARC's assessment is based on its own registry data, and that data is subject to the same degradation that affects everything else ARC holds, the assessment is not reliable in ways nobody currently has the means to audit. ARC might return a lower lending limit than a resident could actually service, because the employment record is corrupted or a dependent has been double-counted. It might return a higher limit than a resident can realistically repay, because a job that no longer exists is still logged as active, or a habitation cost that has risen since the last registry update is still recorded at the old figure. The resident is responsible for the repayment regardless of whether the assessment was accurate. ARC's calculation is the record. The record is the truth.

**When repayment falters**, ARC does not act immediately. A single missed deduction cycle is absorbed — allocation continues, the shortfall is logged, the repayment schedule is extended. A pattern of missed payments triggers managed decline: ARC begins reducing the resident's allocation to recover what it can, compressing their Cyph cycle by cycle while the debt balance continues to accrue. The resident is not frozen. They can still spend, still function, still see the decline coming. The debt does not pause while this is happening. The reduction and the balance compound against each other.

After a defined threshold of missed payments — enough that ARC's own model concludes the debt is unserviceable rather than merely struggling — the system moves to a freeze. The allocation is suspended entirely: no new Cyph in, no spending access out. ARC then notifies the relevant authority (see *Open Follow-Ups* — this body's name and placement within Arbour's institutional structure are to be determined during the Branch deep-dives). That body determines what happens next: restructuring, compelled assessment, tier reclassification, or formal proceedings.

The sequence matters. By the time the freeze arrives, the resident has already been living through managed decline for long enough that the freeze is less a sudden intervention and more a conclusion — the system deciding, formally, what the resident has already known for some time. The freeze is not a punishment for missing a payment. It is the paperwork catching up to a collapse that already happened. In the Sprawl, where ARC's assessment is least reliable and the gap between a serviceable debt and an unserviceable one is narrowest, residents may enter managed decline through no failure of their own — an assessment based on corrupted parameters that was wrong from the beginning, grinding them down on a schedule ARC generated in error. The freeze, when it comes, is the same. The system does not distinguish between a debt it misjudged and one the resident simply couldn't carry. Both look the same in ARC's record. Both end the same way.

People continue to live during freezes — through the off-grid economy, through Otis Audagar's network, through whatever the community can quietly provide — but they are suspended from the formal economy for as long as the process runs, and the process does not run quickly.

### The Minimum Floor

ARC does not reduce a resident's allocation to zero. A floor exists: a minimum allocation intended to maintain basic subsistence — food access, environmental shelter, utilities at survival level. The floor is real.

The floor is not enough for anything beyond it.

It is enough to stay in ARC's registry, to remain nominally registered and theoretically eligible for all the mechanisms of a formal economy that are, in practice, inaccessible at floor-level Cyph. It is enough to survive, which is not the same as enough to live. People at the floor cannot meaningfully accumulate, petition for reclassification, pay an amendment fee, afford the probationary period that Branch employment requires, or do any of the things that the official upward pathways technically remain open to them. They remain in the system — dependent on it, counted by it, subject to all its controls — with none of its capacities.

The gap between the floor and any meaningful motion is the entire architecture of the system. **People at the floor are not outside Arbour's economy. They are its most captive participants.** They are still registered. They are still allocated. They cannot leave.

### Visibility — ARC Records as Public Documents

ARC registry data is publicly requestable. Any registered Arbour resident can submit a request through the Tabularium for another person's record, including their tier movement history, posting history, and current allocation band. The request takes time to process and costs a modest Cyph fee, but access is not restricted by tier.

In practice this means tier movement is visible and legible to anyone who cares to look. A Meridian family researching a prospective marriage partner can pull the record. A Luminary employer reviewing an applicant can request the file. A Sprawl resident curious whether the person who moved up from their district is still there can find out.

The upward movement of the people who made it is inspected and noted. The downward movement of the people who didn't is inspected and noted in a different way.
