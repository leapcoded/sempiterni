# Communications Infrastructure

*Lives in: World & Lore → Core Systems. Companion to Economy and Enforcement (the posting system, the Department of Communications as posting publisher), Arbour Political Systems (the Council's information strategy), Obsidian Branch (surveillance), Violet Branch (cultural authorisation), and The Margin (unofficial information channels).*

---

## The Baseline

Arbour communicates the way it has always communicated: in person, by word of mouth, by written note passed hand to hand. This is not a failure of technology. It is what three centuries of life inside a city whose infrastructure has never extended uniformly across its population has produced — a culture in which oral transmission is the reliable channel and everything else is supplementary, unreliable, or not available to you depending on where you live.

The data pad network exists. It is not a personal messaging system. It is a broadcast infrastructure: what the Council and the Department of Communications want published, published. The distinction matters. A data pad tells you what the official record says. It does not let you tell anyone anything back.

---

## The Data Pad Network

Data pads are fixed terminals — mounted at community hubs, market access points, transit stops, Branch facility entrances — that display the Department of Communications' authorised output: postings, Council announcements, approved public information, employment notices. They do not receive input from ordinary residents. They are not a two-way medium.

Coverage follows the tier gradient precisely:

**Luminary** — dense, current, and reliable. A resident moving through Luminary's main corridors passes a data pad at regular intervals. The information they display is updated frequently and the terminals themselves are maintained. The effect is that official information in Luminary is ambient: it reaches you without you seeking it, it is current when it reaches you, and because everyone around you is receiving the same information through the same infrastructure, the official record functions as a shared frame of reference in ways it does not elsewhere.

**Meridian** — present in main thoroughfares, intermittent in side streets and residential sections. The update frequency is lower and the maintenance less reliable. A Meridian resident who stays off the main routes may go days without encountering a functional data pad. The information gap this creates is partly compensated by oral transmission — neighbours share what they've seen — which means by the time information reaches someone via that route it has been filtered through several other people's understanding of it.

**Sprawl** — sparse, frequently broken, and often running information that is days or weeks out of date. In many Sprawl districts, particularly the oldest and densest, data pad coverage is functionally absent. The community hub board — a physical board where postings and notices are hand-posted — is the primary official information channel, and its contents are only as current as whoever last updated it. In practice, the Sprawl learns most things through people before it learns them through any official channel.

The patchiness of Sprawl coverage is not accidental and is not primarily a budget problem. A data pad that does not reach a Sprawl district is a data pad that cannot carry Council-approved information *into* that district — and equally, cannot carry anything else. The infrastructure gap is simultaneously a reach failure and a control feature. The Department of Communications administers both effects without distinguishing between them in its official mandate.

## How Information Actually Moves

### Within the Hull-Core

In Luminary and upper Meridian, the combination of data pad density and a population that occupies a relatively bounded, well-mapped space means official information circulates quickly and personal information circulates through social channels that are similarly bounded. People know their neighbours, their colleagues, their Branch contacts. Information that enters this network reaches its destination reliably, if not always quickly by the standards of a truly connected system.

Written notes — physical, hand-delivered by a person or left at a known location — remain the standard for anything requiring some degree of privacy or permanence. The culture of written correspondence is old and well-developed in the hull-core; certain communications (formal Branch correspondence, contract arrangements, personal matters of some sensitivity) are conducted in writing as a matter of established practice rather than because no alternative exists. The practice of writing things down carries a specific weight in Arbour: a written record can be produced, queried, held against you. People who put things in writing understand what they are doing. People who don't, don't.

### Through the Sprawl

The Sprawl runs on oral transmission. Information moves person to person, household to household, community to community, through a dense web of neighbourhood relationships, market contacts, workplace connections, and the informal networks that form wherever people share infrastructure — a water access point, a community kitchen, a repair workshop. In a community this densely connected at the human level, oral transmission is fast, wide-reaching, and remarkably accurate over short distances. It also degrades and mutates over longer distances, in proportion to how many transmission steps it crosses and how little context each recipient has to check it against.

The Sprawl's information landscape is therefore simultaneously more connected and less reliable than the hull-core's. A piece of news that originates in Ashwick can reach the far western Sprawl arm within a day through the right chain of contacts, but what arrives there may not be what left Ashwick. The distortion is not usually deliberate — it is the ordinary effect of oral transmission through many hands without a fixed record to anchor it. Deliberate distortion, when it occurs, is harder to detect precisely because the channel already produces noise.

Written notes circulate in the Sprawl too, but the literacy barrier matters here in ways it does not in the hull-core. Not everyone reads, and the assumption that a written note will be legible to its recipient cannot be made. The Sprawl has developed conventions for this: certain kinds of visual marking (colour, symbol, fold pattern) that carry meaning independent of text, readable by anyone who knows the convention. These are not a formal system. They are accumulated community practice, varying by district.

### Across Tiers

Cross-tier communication is structurally uncomfortable and practically slow. The official channels — data pads, job comms, formal Branch correspondence — are legible across tiers but are not personal messaging systems. The unofficial channels — oral transmission, written notes, personal contact — require someone willing to move between tiers, which is not freely available to everyone.

People who cross tiers regularly as part of their work (Branch employees, traders, certain Meridian-tier professionals) are the primary carriers of cross-tier informal information. A Meridian trader who works a Sprawl market knows things that other Meridian residents don't, because they physically move through both spaces. This positional advantage is understood and occasionally cultivated deliberately — Otis Audagar's network is a formalised version of what many people do informally.

## The Department of Communications

The Department of Communications is the body that controls what the data pad network displays. Its official mandate covers posting publication, public announcements, and approved information distribution. Its actual function extends to content curation — decisions about what is published, what is withheld, what framing official announcements carry, and what the silence in the network communicates when something is not published.

The Department's relationship to the Branch structure remains to be formally placed (see Open Follow-Ups). Its functional relationships are with Obsidian Branch (which initiates postings the Department publishes) and with Azure Branch (which maintains the technical infrastructure the Department operates through). Whether it has any formal relationship with Violet Branch — which holds the cultural authorisation mandate — is ambiguous in its current institutional description and may be deliberately so.

The Department does not publicly acknowledge that it makes editorial decisions. Its official position is that it publishes what is submitted to it through proper channels and maintains what the infrastructure can support. Both statements are technically accurate. Neither accounts for what happens to information that never reaches the Department through proper channels, or for infrastructure that is maintained at exactly the level it currently operates at, no more.

## The Control Architecture

The Council's information control operates in two registers that function differently at different tiers.

**Structural control** handles the Sprawl. A data pad network that doesn't reliably reach most Sprawl districts means that Council-approved information doesn't reach most Sprawl districts either — but it also means that anything the Council has not approved also doesn't reach them through official channels. The Sprawl's information environment is therefore shaped by what oral transmission produces: local, community-specific, often accurate about immediate conditions and often uninformed about anything beyond immediate conditions. This suits the Council's purposes without requiring active maintenance. The gap does the work.

**Active control** handles the hull-core, where the infrastructure is good enough that information could flow freely if it were permitted to. In Luminary and upper Meridian, the Department of Communications makes real editorial decisions about what the data pad network displays and how. Council announcements are curated. Certain developments are framed before they are published. Some things are not published at all — not suppressed in a way that creates a visible absence, but simply not entered into the official channel, leaving residents to eventually receive them through social transmission in a form the Department has not shaped. By the time official silence is noticed, the thing not said has already been replaced by whatever the social channel produced in its absence.

The effect is that the hull-core's information environment is more controlled than the Sprawl's, despite better infrastructure — because control requires infrastructure to operate through. The Sprawl cannot be controlled in the same way because the infrastructure to control it doesn't exist. What exists instead is a managed isolation: the Sprawl knows what its community networks produce, and not much more.

## Unofficial Channels

**The rumour network** — the oral transmission layer that operates below and alongside everything official — is not a single network and does not have a name. It is the aggregate of every conversation, every piece of news passed between neighbours, every observation shared at a water point or a market stall. It carries accurate information, distorted information, deliberate misinformation, and everything between, without marking which is which. Its most important feature, from the Council's perspective, is that it is uncontrollable in the Sprawl and largely self-regulating in the hull-core, where social consequences for spreading inaccurate information in well-connected communities are real enough to create informal correction mechanisms.

**The tunnel network** — ARBOUR|05's original maintenance infrastructure, now The Margin's primary transit channel — functions as a secondary physical information channel. Material, people, and messages move through it outside the official sight lines. What travels through the tunnels reaches its destination without passing through any data pad or community hub board. This is its value to The Margin and its threat to Obsidian Branch, which has limited but non-zero visibility into the tunnel network's use.

**Carrier relationships** — individuals who move between territories that don't otherwise connect. Wayfarers who occasionally make it into Arbour's trading orbit. Shadow settlement figures who pass between the city's outer edge and the wider Badlands. Otis Audagar's informant network, which has eyes in the shadow settlements that Arbour's official infrastructure never reaches. These are not a communications system so much as occasional, unreliable, but sometimes irreplaceable channels for information that cannot travel any other route.
