# Cordis Weather Systems

*Lives in: World & Lore → Core Systems. Companion to Arbour City Geography (the city's relationship to its physical environment), Arbour Power (atmospheric energy harvesters running on high-altitude wind), and Badlands Ecology (the Badlands' aridity as a regional feature, not a planetary one). Upstream of any future Communities of Cordis document — the weather and stellar system established here applies planet-wide, with regional variation.*

---

## The KOI-8565 System

KOI-8565 is a binary system: one primary star and one smaller companion, gravitationally bound, orbited by Cordis at a distance that places it well within the system's combined habitable zone.

**The primary** is a G-type star — slightly smaller and cooler than Sol, producing a warm yellow-white light that reads to human eyes as ordinary daylight, close enough to Earth-baseline that the founding generation adapted without difficulty. Slightly less UV output than Sol, slightly more orange at the warm end of its spectrum. Cordis's primary is the dominant light source in the sky by a significant margin.

**The companion** is a K-dwarf — a smaller, cooler star that appears in Cordis's sky as a visibly smaller disc, a fraction of the primary's apparent size. Its light has a distinctly warmer, more amber-orange quality than the primary's. On its own it would be a gentle light; against the primary it reads as a secondary presence — noticeable, warm-toned, producing its own distinct shadows when above the horizon at a sufficient angle from the primary.

Cordis orbits both stars together in a stable circumbinary orbit — the same configuration as some real binary systems, where the planet circles the centre of mass of the pair rather than either star individually. This orbit is stable across long timescales. The two suns never eclipse each other from Cordis's surface.

## Day and Year

**Day length: 26 hours.** Close enough to Earth-baseline that the founding generation's circadian rhythms required minimal adjustment; different enough that the extra two hours per day are genuinely felt over the course of a year. Three centuries of Cordis-born generations have fully adapted. They do not experience a 26-hour day as anything other than the day.

**Year length: approximately 390 Cordis days** — equivalent to just over 423 Earth days, or roughly 1.16 Earth years. Long enough that seasons are extended relative to Earth equivalents; short enough that the pattern is still legible as a seasonal cycle without feeling foreign. The founding generation's documentation of seasons was based on Earth-calendar equivalents and has never been formally reconciled with the Cordis year; practical seasonal reckoning in Arbour and the Free Territories is based on observable conditions rather than any formal calendar.

## Light and Shadow

The twin-sun sky is not dramatic in the way a first encounter with the concept might suggest. Most of the time — when the companion is at a similar angle to the primary, or when it has not yet risen or has already set — Cordis's sky simply looks like a sky with one sun in it. The companion's smaller disc and its amber-warm quality are present and visible, but the primary dominates.

Where the effect becomes distinctive is when both suns are above the horizon at different angles. In these conditions everything on Cordis casts two shadows: a sharp, dark primary shadow cast by the dominant sun, and a fainter, slightly offset secondary shadow cast by the companion. The secondary shadow is never as crisp or as dark as the primary — it reads as a blurred copy, displaced by the angular separation between the two suns. In strong midday light the effect is subtle. In lower light — early morning, late afternoon, overcast conditions — the double shadow becomes more pronounced and easier to notice.

The light Cordis receives is a blend of the two spectral outputs: yellow-white primary plus amber companion producing an overall daylight quality that is slightly warmer than Sol's equivalent. This is not visible as "wrong" to anyone born on Cordis; it is simply what light looks like. The founding generation noted it in their records as warmer and marginally more golden than Earth daylight. Three centuries later, nobody compares it to anything.

The companion's position relative to the primary shifts across the year as both stars follow their mutual orbit and Cordis moves around them. There are periods when the companion rises and sets close to the primary, producing minimal double-shadow effect, and periods when their angular separation is greatest and the double shadows are most marked. These periods correspond loosely to specific seasons, but not precisely — the stellar and planetary cycles are not synchronised in a way that produces clean alignment with the seasonal pattern.

## Seasons on Arbour's Continent

Cordis has axial tilt and a genuine seasonal cycle. On Arbour's continent — the continent the city sits on, in the mid-latitude zone — four seasons are distinct and practically significant.

### Wet Season

The heaviest rainfall of the year. Sustained, often intense precipitation driven by warm air masses from the ocean meeting cooler inland air. Storms are common and can be severe — high winds, significant rain over days rather than hours, periodic flooding in the Sprawl's low-lying areas where the debris field ground's drainage is poor. The atmospheric energy harvesters at altitude run at or near peak capacity during wet season; the near-constant high-velocity upper-atmosphere wind the power document already establishes is most reliable and strongest during this period.

For the Sprawl, wet season is practically difficult. Open-air construction without proper drainage floods. The community infrastructure — market areas, water access points, the outdoor elements of daily life — is disrupted. Hull-core residents, better sheltered, notice wet season mainly as light quality and sound.

The wet season sky is frequently overcast, reducing the twin-sun light to a diffuse grey-white. Double shadows are largely absent. The companion's disc is occasionally visible through broken cloud as a warmer, amber shape — distinguishable from the primary by colour alone in these conditions.

### Growing Season

Warm, variable, the most hospitable stretch of the year. Rainfall tapers from wet season to occasional, temperatures rise, and the long Cordis days (already 26 hours; in growing season somewhat longer due to axial tilt) provide sustained warmth that Verdant Branch's agricultural operations are designed around. The agricultural calendar is calibrated to growing season with a precision that the rest of Arbour's infrastructure does not match.

Weather in growing season is variable within its warmth — storms are less severe than wet season but not absent, wind patterns shift with the twin-sun heating geometry, and the day-to-day conditions change faster than a single-star world's equivalent would. The double-shadow effect is most common in growing season, when clear skies and the two suns' varying positions produce the angular separations that make both shadows distinct.

Growing season is when Badlands travellers move most freely. The moderate corridor is at its most productive. Aeolian Wayfarer caravans range furthest during this period.

### Dry Season

Hot, minimal rainfall, the character of the Badlands intensified and extended. Temperatures on Arbour's continent peak in dry season; the Sprawl's open areas, without the hull-core's structural shielding, experience heat that makes certain hours of the day impractical for outdoor work. Water management becomes the central practical concern — Verdant Branch's water recycling documentation notes the seasonal strain on the system during this period.

The atmospheric harvesters' performance drops in dry season. The upper-atmosphere winds are less consistent, heat-driven atmospheric instability produces less reliably directed airflow, and the harvesters that run on the near-constant high-altitude wind see their output drop. This is one of the seasonal power fluctuations that affects Arbour's grid without the Council acknowledging it publicly.

The sky in dry season is characteristically clear and bright — the hard, flat light of a high-sun day with minimal cloud, the primary's disc at its most intense, the companion readable as a slightly softer secondary presence at its side. The double shadows in dry season are sharp and hot.

### Cold Season

The shortest stretch and the least extreme. Axial tilt takes Arbour's continent partially away from the primary; days shorten; temperatures fall to the cooler end of tolerable without reaching anything that would qualify as a genuine winter by Earth standards. Rainfall returns, lighter than wet season, inconsistent. Wind picks up again, colder in character than wet season's warm-air storms.

Cold season is when the companion is most visually distinctive relative to the primary — the lower light angle and the companion's warmer amber quality produce a sky where the two suns are most easily read as different things, particularly in morning and evening when both are near the horizon and their colour separation is most apparent. The double shadows in cold season are long, faint, and angled in ways that are sometimes genuinely disorienting in unfamiliar environments.

Cold season is the quiet period in the Badlands. Wayfarer caravan movement slows. Installation monitoring activity by Scarlet Branch reduces — not because the sites change, but because the terrain is harder and the extraction operations that share the same territory become impractical.

## The Upper Atmosphere

The power document establishes that the atmospheric energy harvesters run on near-constant high-velocity wind at altitude. This is a real feature of Cordis's atmosphere above Arbour's continent — the twin-sun heating produces consistent upper-level airflow that is more reliable and more energetic than Earth's equivalent wind patterns at similar altitudes. The atmospheric harvesters tap this. It does not translate directly to surface conditions: ground-level wind is variable and seasonal, not the consistent high-altitude resource the harvesters use.

The interaction between upper-atmosphere wind patterns and surface conditions produces part of the weather variability that characterises Arbour's continent. Upper-level disturbances occasionally translate down to surface storms with more speed and intensity than ground-level conditions alone would suggest. Experienced weather-readers in Badlands communities and among Aeolian Wayfarers track upper-atmosphere signals — cloud formation at altitude, the quality of the wind shear at the margin between weather layers — as part of practical navigation and camp-selection.
