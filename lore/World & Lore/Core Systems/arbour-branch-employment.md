## Part One — The Vocation

Branch membership in Arbour is not employment. It is identity.

ARBOUR|05 launched with a crew — engineers, navigators, medics, agronomists, administrators — each with a role that the ship's survival depended on, each bound to that role by something closer to vocation than contract. Three centuries of city life have not dissolved that founding structure. They have calcified it. The Branches are what the crew became when the voyage ended and the roles needed somewhere else to go.

The result is that a Branch member does not think of themselves as working for the Azure Branch the way a person might work for an employer. They *are* Azure Branch. Their training, their relationships, their social standing, their ARC record, their sense of who they are — all of it is filtered through Branch membership in a way that makes the distinction between person and institution largely invisible from the inside.

This means failure carries a specific weight. A Branch member who makes a serious error has not underperformed. They have let down their crew — the people who trained them, sponsored them, stood in a ceremony and heard them take an oath. The shame of this is distinct from the formal consequence, and often more lasting.

It also means that leaving is not resignation. It is something closer to abandonment of post.

## Part Two — Entry

### Examination

Each Branch sets its own examination, designed and administered internally rather than through the education body. The standards reflect what each Branch considers essential — and since each Branch has spent three centuries deciding what that means, the examinations carry the weight of accumulated institutional judgment rather than any external framework.

**Azure Branch** examination is the most technically demanding — practical and written both, testing genuine procedural competence rather than memorised answers. A candidate who can recite the correct sequence but cannot execute it under assessment conditions does not pass.

**Obsidian Branch** examination has an aptitude and psychological dimension alongside the technical. The Branch is selecting for people it can trust with significant discretionary power, and the examination reflects that. What exactly it tests is not publicly detailed.

**Violet Branch** weights demonstrated achievement differently — a portfolio of cultural work, recognition within a discipline, evidence of the kind of talent the Branch intends to develop and deploy. More subjective than other examinations, which gives existing members more influence over outcomes than the formal process implies.

**Golden, Scarlet, and other Branches** each run their own equivalent processes, shaped by their specific mandates and internal cultures. The principle is consistent: the Branch decides what it needs, and the examination tests for it.

Failing examination is not publicly shaming in the way a posting is. You simply did not make it through. Candidates may in principle try again, though the window in which a second attempt is plausible narrows quickly as a candidate ages out of the profile each Branch is selecting for.

### Sponsorship

No candidate sits examination without a sponsor — a current Branch member of sufficient standing who has identified them as a suitable candidate and is willing to formally vouch for them. The sponsor's name is attached to the candidate's application and remains attached to their record throughout their career.

This is not a formality. If the candidate fails to meet the Branch's standards — in examination, in probation, or at any point in their subsequent career — the failure reflects on the sponsor. A sponsored candidate who brings the Branch into disrepute has brought their sponsor with them. Senior members are therefore selective. Sponsorship is an act of genuine commitment, not a favour, and the social cost of sponsoring someone who turns out to be unsuitable is real enough that the wrong candidate will not find a willing sponsor regardless of their examination results.

In practice this creates a self-replicating system. The people best positioned to find sponsors are the people already inside Branch networks — candidates from Branch families, candidates who came through the education tracks that Branch members informally oversee, candidates who are already known quantities to the people whose reputation is at stake. A Sprawl candidate with no existing Branch connections and no family history in the system has to find a sponsor who is willing to take a risk on someone outside their known circle. This happens. It is not common.

### Probation

A candidate who passes examination enters a probationary period — working alongside full Branch members, assessed continuously, paid at a rate below full Branch allocation. The probationary period is when the Branch decides whether the examination result was accurate.

The below-full-rate pay during probation is the mechanism by which the Cyph barrier operates. A candidate who scraped together enough Cyph to make it to examination and through it may not have enough reserve to sustain themselves through a probationary period that could last months. The Branch does not consider this its problem. The expectation is that a serious candidate will have planned for it — which is to say, the expectation assumes a level of financial cushion that Sprawl candidates rarely have.

Some probationary candidates wash out. This is treated as a normal part of the selection process, handled quietly within the Branch, and carries no formal mark on the individual's record beyond noting that probation was not completed. It is a different category of outcome from formal departure.

### Induction

Candidates who complete probation successfully are inducted. The ceremony is shared across all Branches — a moment of formal crossing from candidate to crew, marked by an oath, witnessed by Branch seniors, and conducted in language that has retained ship terminology across three centuries of use. *Crew. Station. Post. Service.* The words are worn smooth but they are still the words.

The ceremony carries genuine weight. Branch members who have been inducted for decades remember theirs. The day you became crew is the day your professional identity formed, and the oath you took is not rhetorical.

Branch-specific elements are layered on top — different Branches mark the occasion differently, with particular traditions, particular seniors who must be present, particular forms the oath takes within the shared structure. Azure Branch inductions are reportedly the most austere. Violet Branch's are the most elaborate.

## Part Three — Inside the Branch

### Advancement

Advancement within a Branch runs on a combination of demonstrated merit and relationship. Neither alone is sufficient.

Merit that nobody senior has noticed or chosen to advocate for does not advance a career. Advocacy from a sponsor or senior member that is not backed by genuine competence becomes a liability for the advocate when it becomes visible. The two are entangled — performance gives sponsors reason to advocate, and advocacy gives performance somewhere to go.

The sponsor relationship does not end at induction. A sponsor who has put their reputation behind a candidate continues to have a stake in that person's trajectory and continues to advocate — or not — as the candidate's record justifies. Senior Branch members accumulate a cohort of people they have sponsored, and the collective performance of that cohort is part of what their own standing reflects. Branch politics, viewed from this angle, is feudal: networks of mutual obligation running up and down, reputation flowing both ways between senior and junior, loyalty made structurally rational by the fact that letting your network down damages everyone in it.

### Legacy

Branch membership runs in families. Not by law — no regulation requires an Azure Branch engineer's child to enter Azure Branch — but by deep social expectation and structural advantage. A child who grows up in a Branch family absorbs the specific preparation for that Branch's examination before they know they're being prepared. They know the right people. They have a natural sponsor already in place. The examination they take is one their family has been quietly helping them towards for years.

The result is that Branch membership calcified alongside the tier system and from the same root. The descendants of ARBOUR|05's original crew roles are, broadly, the descendants of the people who hold those roles now. Not without exception. Not without the occasional outsider who made it through on exceptional merit and found a sponsor willing to take the risk. But broadly, consistently, generation after generation.

## Part Four — Departure

### Voluntary Exit

Leaving a Branch voluntarily is formally possible. It happens rarely, and the rarity is structural rather than regulatory — there is no rule preventing it, but the social and practical consequences are significant enough that most members who reach a senior level find the cost of leaving higher than the cost of staying.

A voluntary departure is recorded permanently in the individual's ARC record. There is no infraction attached, no posting, no Cyph action — the departure itself carries no formal punishment. What it carries is visibility. Anyone who pulls the record can see it. In a culture where Branch membership is vocation and identity, the mark speaks without annotation: this person left their crew. The context of why is not in the record. The fact of it is.

Junior members who leave early in their careers find the mark easier to absorb — a probationary departure reads as simply not having been the right fit. A senior member's voluntary departure reads differently. There is no civilian life that a senior Branch member steps naturally into. The skills, the network, the identity — all of it was Branch-shaped. What remains outside it is a record with a gap and a community of former colleagues who do not quite know how to account for you.

### Quiet Removal

Distinct from voluntary departure, and more common at senior levels, is the institution handling a problem internally without creating a formal record of departure at all.

When a Branch member becomes inconvenient — knows something they shouldn't, has objected to something in ways that can't be absorbed, has become a liability the Branch cannot manage through ordinary means — the institution has mechanisms that do not involve the posting system, do not produce an ARC departure record, and do not leave the kind of visible mark that a voluntary exit creates. The member is redirected. Reassigned. Their file is updated to reflect a career transition rather than a departure. What appears in the record is a different role, not an absence.

This is more sinister than formal departure precisely because it is cleaner. A voluntary departure is visible. A quiet removal is not. The person is still in the record. They are simply somewhere else in it, doing something that poses no further problem, with no annotation explaining why the change happened.

Wren Emberlain's record is the canonical example of this mechanism at its most complete.

## Part Five — The Medical Branch

### The Collapse of Public Provision

The Ivory Branch retains its name, its council seat, and its formal existence. What it no longer retains is a functioning pipeline.

The examination process technically exists. There are not enough senior members remaining with the standing and knowledge to sponsor candidates, run rigorous assessments, and perform inductions that mean what inductions are supposed to mean. The knowledge required to pass the examination has itself degraded over generations — each cohort of practitioners trained from a slightly thinner base than the one before it, producing examiners who can only test for what they themselves know, which is already less than what their teachers knew. Azure Branch, Obsidian Branch, and others compete for the same educated pool of candidates and offer better Cyph, better prospects, and a Branch that will still exist in thirty years.

The formal pipeline has effectively stopped. The last properly inducted Ivory Branch members are ageing. They know what they are — the final generation of something that began with the ship's original medical staff and has been thinning ever since. Some of them hold the council seat. Some of them know that when they are gone, the seat will be held by someone whose credentials are, in the formal sense, incomplete.

### What Persists

Medicine has not vanished from Arbour's upper tiers. What has vanished is the formal Branch structure that once organised and legitimised it.

A small number of practitioners still operate in Luminary and upper Meridian — some with formal induction behind them, some who were trained informally by the last fully qualified practitioners and never went through a ceremony that the Branch could no longer properly run. They serve the upper tiers through arrangements that are closer to private practice than Branch service. Their knowledge is real but narrower than what the founding medical staff carried. They do not advertise the incompleteness of their credentials. Their patients do not ask.

For everyone below Meridian, this arrangement is invisible and irrelevant. The Sprawl's medical provision — always thin, always under-resourced — has not meaningfully changed. The self-taught healers and plant medicine that serve the Sprawl now are what have always served the Sprawl. The formal Branch's collapse is a Luminary story, not a Sprawl one. The Sprawl never had enough of it to notice its absence.
