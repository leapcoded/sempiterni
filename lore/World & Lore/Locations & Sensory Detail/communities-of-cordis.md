## Overview

Cordis is populated across two continents by communities whose common ancestry — the crash of ARBOUR|05 three hundred years ago — is, for most of them, either mythologised, unknown, or simply not the thing that organises daily life. The diversity of communities that has emerged from that single origin point is the product of geography, Aetheris exposure, the different survival problems different territories presented, and three centuries of people making choices and passing the consequences of those choices to their children.

The two continents are substantially different in character. Arbour's continent is marked by the crash site and by the Aetheris-affected geography the crash produced or intensified. The Wayfarer continent is older-feeling in the ecological sense — more varied, more intact, with regions of genuine lushness that have no equivalent on Arbour's side of the ocean. The difference is not purely climatic. It is the difference between a landscape shaped by catastrophe and a landscape that has simply been itself for longer than anyone remembers.

## Arbour's Continent

### Arbour

The city. Three hundred years old, built on and around the crash site, housing the majority of Arbour's continent's human population. Its internal geography — hull-core, Meridian, Sprawl — is documented extensively elsewhere. Its relationship to the rest of the continent is one of deliberate disconnection: officially, there is nothing of interest beyond Arbour's walls; officially, the city is self-sufficient; officially, the territory outside its administrative boundary is dangerous and ungoverned. None of these are accurate, but they serve the Council's purposes well enough to have been maintained for generations.

### Shadow Settlements

A scattered arc of informal communities between Arbour's outer territory and the deeper Badlands — close enough to the city to trade with it (through Otis Audagar's network and the grey-zone economy), far enough to operate outside the Council's direct reach. Shadow settlements are not organised collectively; each is its own thing, with its own character, leadership structure (or lack of one), and relationship to both Arbour and the Badlands communities further out.

They are, collectively, the margin where Arbour's administrative reach ends and everything else begins. People who have left Arbour voluntarily or been effectively expelled from it, people who were never inside it to begin with, people who work the resource extraction operations Golden Branch runs through its shadow supply network — these are shadow settlement populations. They are also the first thing Aran encounters on arriving at Arbour's continent from the ocean, and his entry into Arbour's social world runs through them.

### Badlands Communities

Non-Wayfarer nomadic and semi-nomadic groups operating in the Badlands — in the moderate corridor between hot zones, in the elevated rocky margin at Arbour's western edge, and in the deeper desert terrain beyond. These are the communities with the most granular practical knowledge of specific Installation sites, accumulated through generations of proximity, and the communities from which Scarlet Branch's candidate sourcing operation draws without their knowledge.

Some Badlands communities have a distant ancestral connection to the Wayfarer divergence — early non-settling groups whose descendants went into the Badlands rather than toward the coast, and have stayed there. Others have no clear Wayfarer lineage; their oral histories tell different stories of origin that may or may not correspond to anything in the historical record. The distinction matters less in practice than the shared reality of living in difficult territory outside any institutional framework.

Cassan Vale's birth community represents one specific type: small, settled rather than nomadic, organised around proximity to a specific Installation cluster — the devotional-practice model that treats Installation sites as spiritually significant rather than practically hazardous. This type exists in several variations across the Badlands, always small, always organised around a specific site. They are among the most Aetheris-exposed communities on Arbour's continent, without institutional framework to understand or manage what that means.

### The Coastal Strip

The narrow territory between Arbour's effective reach and the ocean — home to the crossing communities that maintain the ocean transit tradition. Small, maritime-specialist, operating largely outside Arbour's administrative sight. They have existed long enough that their relationship to Arbour is one of managed mutual awareness: the Council knows they're there and finds them more useful than troublesome, given that their trading connections with the Wayfarer continent occasionally produce intelligence (filtered through Otis Audagar's network) that Arbour's institutions cannot gather any other way.

## The Wayfarer Continent

The second continent is larger than Arbour's in terms of habitable area, more ecologically varied, and, crucially, free of the Aetheris intensification that has been building on Arbour's continent since the crash. Aetheris here is near-zero except in the vicinity of the handful of Installation sites that exist on this continent — sites the Wayfarers generally avoid, and that no institution has systematically studied.

The continent's geography includes genuine lushness: river valleys that support dense, varied plant life, coastal regions with productive marine ecology, highland plateaus with a climate quite unlike anything on Arbour's side. It also includes difficult terrain — dry interior regions, exposed highland ground, areas where the ecology is simply harsh by any standard. The Wayfarers range across a significant portion of it; the fixed settlements occupy the most hospitable pockets.

### Wayfarer Communities

The Wayfarers are the largest organised human presence on the continent, though "organised" is a term they would complicate. The three branches relevant to Book One — Aeolian, Thessaly, Corvane — are detailed in their own documents. The broader Wayfarer culture encompasses additional branches not yet named, each with their own ranging territory and philosophical emphasis, all sharing the foundational principles: movement as survival strategy, authority from demonstrated wisdom, oral tradition as the record.

Wayfarer territory is not fixed or exclusive. The branches range, overlap, and occasionally conflict over resources or routes without the conflicts becoming permanent. Their relationship to the fixed settlements is wary rather than hostile — they are not the same thing, do not want the same things, but share enough practical interests (trade, mutual warning of hazards, occasional medical assistance in either direction) to maintain workable relations in most cases.

The continent's Wayfarer population is spread thinly across a large area. The branches do not gather except for specific purposes — seasonal meetings, significant decisions, ceremonies that the oral tradition marks as requiring community presence. The rest of the time, each caravan is effectively its own world, connected to the larger culture through shared story and practice rather than proximity.

### Fixed Settlements

Scattered in the continent's most hospitable locations — sheltered river valleys, coastal positions with reliable fresh water, plateau edges that combine defensive advantage with access to productive terrain. Fixed settlements are not numerous and are not large. They are, in most cases, communities that diverged from the early crossing groups and chose a different answer to the question of how to survive: staying in one good place rather than ranging from it.

The fixed settlements have developed in substantially different directions depending on their geography and their founding choices. Some are primarily agricultural — making the most of a productive location, building the kind of accumulated material wealth that only comes from staying put. Some are built around craft specialisation: boatbuilding on the coast, specific material processing in locations with the right raw materials, knowledge trades that require library-like accumulation of record. Some are simply villages, doing the ordinary work of keeping people alive in a particular place, without a defining specialisation.

Their relationship to the Wayfarers ranges from genuinely warm to coldly transactional depending on local history. Fixed settlements are a reliable trade partner and an occasional refuge for Wayfarers who need something a caravan cannot provide (medical care for a serious injury, a place to winter with someone too old or ill to range safely). Wayfarers are a reliable information network and an occasional source of materials the settlement cannot produce locally. The exchange works without either side fully trusting the other's way of life.

Their internal governance varies as much as their geography. Some fixed settlements have developed something resembling formal leadership structures — elders, councils, rotating decisions. Some run on more informal consensus. None have developed anything resembling the Council's institutional apparatus; the scale doesn't require it and the cultural inheritance from the Wayfarer divergence makes concentrated authority instinctively uncomfortable even for people who chose settled life.

### Non-Wayfarer Nomadic Groups

Between the Wayfarers and the fixed settlements are groups that fit neither category cleanly — nomadic or semi-nomadic communities that are not part of the Wayfarer cultural tradition (do not share its branches, its naming, its specific epistemology) but also do not stay put. These groups are the least documented in any existing record, because they have the least contact with anyone who would document them.

Some have oral histories that trace back to the crossing — groups that arrived, moved inland, and diverged enough from the Wayfarer mainstream to stop being legible as Wayfarer without clearly becoming something else. Some have oral histories that tell a completely different story of origin, one that has been elaborated across generations into something internally coherent that may or may not correspond to any recoverable historical fact. All of them are simply communities managing survival in the terrain they occupy, by the methods that work there.

Their relationship to the Wayfarers is the least resolved aspect of the continental ecosystem. Some Wayfarer branches have long-standing contact with specific non-Wayfarer nomadic groups in their ranging territory — trade relationships, shared knowledge of specific sites, periodic alliance in response to specific threats. Others have little contact or actively avoid it. The history of specific inter-group relationships on this continent is not recorded anywhere.

## What Arbour Knows

Very little, and most of that is wrong in one direction or another.

Arbour's official position is that the Wayfarer continent exists (this is not suppressed — the ocean crossing is known) and that it is populated by nomadic groups who have chosen a primitive mode of life outside civilisation's reach. The framing serves the Council's self-presentation as the only meaningful form of organised human society on Cordis. The practical reality — that the Wayfarer continent supports a diverse, functioning, and in some respects more ecologically sustainable set of human communities than Arbour does — is not information the Council has any interest in propagating.

The crossing communities know more than Arbour does, and Otis Audagar knows more than the crossing communities share officially. What actually crosses the ocean in the direction of knowledge — stories, practical ecological information, the seed varieties that reach Arbour's black market — is more substantial than Arbour's official position acknowledges.
